How to Read the Option Structures

7Options Structures Legend

Every structure listed in this report is defined-risk only — your maximum loss is known before you enter. Here is what each term means and when the report recommends it.

📘 RSI (Relative Strength Index)

A 0–100 momentum gauge using 14 days of price data. Below 30 = selling pressure may be exhausted (oversold). Above 70 = buying pressure may be exhausted (overbought). Not a standalone signal — combine with price structure, moving averages, and a catalyst check.

📘 Bollinger %B

Measures where price sits within its Bollinger Band (20-day, 2 standard deviations). %B > 1.0 = price above the upper band (stretched high). %B < 0 = price below the lower band (stretched low). Adds confirmation when RSI also signals the same direction.

📘 Z-Score vs 50-day SMA

How many standard deviations (SDs) price is above/below its 50-day average. ±2 SD = notable stretch; ±3 SD = extreme. Used to rank candidates when multiple names are all RSI-overbought — the highest Z-score is most stretched.

📘 Implied Volatility (IV)

The option market's estimate of how much a stock might move. High IV = options are expensive (premiums are fat). Low IV = options are cheap (premiums are thin). High IV favors selling premium (credit spreads). Low IV favors buying premium (debit spreads).

📗 Bull Put Spread (oversold + high IV)

Sell a put at a higher strike, buy a put at a lower strike. You collect premium. Max loss = spread width minus premium collected. Best when you expect a stock to stay above the short put. Defined-risk — you know your max loss before entry.

📗 Bull Call Spread (oversold + low/mod IV)

Buy a call at a lower strike, sell a call at a higher strike. You pay a debit. Max loss = debit paid. Best when you expect a stock to move up but want limited cost. Defined-risk — you can only lose what you paid.

📕 Bear Call Spread (overbought + high IV)

Sell a call at a lower strike, buy a call at a higher strike. You collect premium. Max loss = spread width minus premium collected. Best when you expect a stock to stay below the short call. Defined-risk.

📕 Bear Put Spread (overbought + low/mod IV)

Buy a put at a higher strike, sell a put at a lower strike. You pay a debit. Max loss = debit paid. Best when you expect a stock to drop but want limited cost. Defined-risk.

📗 Cash-Secured Put

Sell a put and hold enough cash to buy 100 shares if assigned. Generates income if the stock stays above the strike. Risk: you may be assigned shares at the strike price — only use on stocks you'd be willing to own at that price. Defined-risk in that shares have limited downside vs zero.

⚠ Paper Trade First

All structures in this report should be practiced in a paper (simulated) account before risking real money. Options involve substantial risk; you can lose more than you expect even with "defined-risk" spreads if you are unfamiliar with assignment, early exercise, or margin requirements. Always confirm with your broker.

Composite scoring method: Candidates are ranked by a composite of: (1) RSI extreme vs 30/70 threshold, (2) Bollinger %B outside the bands, (3) distance from 50-day and 200-day SMA, (4) Z-score vs 50-day mean, and (5) consecutive up/down days relative to ATR. A name showing extremes on 3+ of these measures ranks above a name that is only RSI-extreme. The reversion-vs-ruin filter (Section 8) is applied after the technical ranking.
Risk Filter & Methodology

8Guardrails — Reversion vs. Ruin & Caveats

The most important step in this scan is separating a name that is temporarily stretched from one that is permanently broken. Here is how to think about it.

Reject these as reversion setups (falling knives / structural breaks):
  • Fraud, going-concern doubt, or accounting restatement underway
  • Dividend cut driving income investors to exit permanently
  • Failed drug trial (biotech) or debt blowup — the business model is impaired
  • Buyout/merger repricing — stock is now pinned to a deal price, not its mean
  • Drop of 50%+ in days with no stabilization — likely more to come
  • Bankruptcy filing or near-certain default — RSI doesn't save you here
Keep these as reversion candidates (sentiment/positioning excess):
  • Structurally sound business hit by a macro shock that is identifiably reversible
  • Price near a prior support/resistance level with a defined "mean" to revert to
  • RSI/Z-score extreme driven by crowded positioning (sector rotation, geopolitical spike)
  • The "why it moved" is sentiment-driven, not a fundamental re-rating
Special handling caveats:
  • Long-vol ETPs (VXX, UVXY, VIXY): Only fade when stretched UP (spike). Never buy as "cheap" — structural decay grinds them to zero over time. A low RSI is decay, not a setup.
  • Leveraged ETFs (UCO, SCO, TBT, TQQQ/SQQQ, etc.): Volatility decay + roll costs mean they deviate from any long-run mean. Use for short-term tactical trades only (days to 1–2 weeks). For multi-week holds, use the unleveraged equivalent.
  • K-1 tax forms: USO, UNG, UGA, UCO, DBC, BOIL, most CurrencyShares ETFs issue a Schedule K-1 at tax time, which complicates tax filing. Prefer no-K-1 alternatives (XLE for oil, PDBC for broad commodities, BCI/COMB) where the exposure allows.
  • Proxy ETFs (GDX, XOP, OIH, URA, COPX, SIL): These track companies, not the underlying commodity.

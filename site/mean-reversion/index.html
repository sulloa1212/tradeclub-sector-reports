7Options Structures Legend

This section explains how to think about matching a setup to a structure — it is generic method, not a recommendation for any specific trade today. Always paper-trade first and confirm data before using real capital.

Oversold + High IV → Premium Selling

When a stock is beaten down AND implied volatility (IV, the price of options) is elevated, you can sell expensive options to collect premium while positioning for a bounce. Examples: cash-secured puts (you agree to buy the stock at a lower price and collect premium), put credit spreads (sell a put, buy a lower-strike put to cap risk). Both are defined-risk.

Oversold + Low IV → Buying

When options are cheap AND the stock is oversold, you can buy options (options are inexpensive when IV is low). Example: bull call spread — buy a call at or near the stock price, sell a higher-strike call to reduce cost. Defined-risk: maximum loss = what you paid.

Overbought + High IV → Premium Selling (Bearish)

When a stock is stretched up AND IV is high, sell expensive calls. Example: call credit spread (sell a call, buy a higher-strike call to cap your risk). The collected premium is your max gain; the spread width minus premium is your max loss. Always defined-risk.

Overbought + Low IV → Buying (Bearish)

When options are cheap AND the stock looks stretched up, buy puts. Example: bear put spread — buy a put near the stock price, sell a lower-strike put to reduce cost. Defined-risk: max loss = what you paid for the spread.

Post-Earnings / Binary Event

After an earnings report, IV typically collapses (called an "IV crush"). This hurts option buyers and helps option sellers. When IV is very high right after an earnings gap (like NFLX today), wait for IV to settle before initiating a directional play — or use very short-dated defined-risk spreads to limit theta and vega exposure.

What the Gauges Mean

RSI (Relative Strength Index, 0–100): below 30 = oversold (sellers may be exhausted); above 70 = overbought (buyers may be exhausted). Extreme: below 20 / above 80. Bollinger %B: below 0 = price below the lower band (stretched down); above 1 = above upper band (stretched up). Z-score: how many standard deviations price is from its mean — above 2 or below -2 is statistically unusual. Williams %R: below -80 = oversold; above -20 = overbought.

Leveraged, Futures-Based & Proxy ETF Rules

  • Long-vol ETPs (VXX, UVXY): Only trade when stretched UP on a volatility spike — fade the spike. Never buy because RSI is low; the contango roll cost of ~5% per month drives 60–80% annual decay for long products like UVXY. Low RSI = structural drift, not a setup.
  • Leveraged ETFs (SOXS, UCO, BOIL, TQQQ, etc.): Use for short-term tactical reversion only — never hold multi-week. Daily rebalancing math causes volatility drag that diverges from any long-run mean.
  • K-1 tax forms: USO, UNG, UGA, UCO, BOIL, DBC, and most futures-based commodity ETFs issue a Schedule K-1 at tax time (complex for retail traders). Prefer K-1-free alternatives: PDBC or COMB for broad commodities, BNO for Brent crude.
  • Proxy ETFs (GDX, GDX, XOP, OIH, COPX): These track companies that produce a commodity, not the commodity itself. They carry equity beta and can diverge sharply from the underlying spot price — always noted in the tables above.

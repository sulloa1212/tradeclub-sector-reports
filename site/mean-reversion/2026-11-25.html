8Options Structures Legend

This legend explains what each educational structure means and when to consider it. These are starting-point concepts — not recommendations to trade. Always paper-trade first.

Oversold + High IV → Premium Sellers

Bull put spread: Sell a lower put, buy an even lower put. You collect premium if the stock stays above your short strike. Max loss is defined (the spread width minus credit collected). Best when IV is high (more premium to collect) and you believe the stock won't fall much further.

Oversold + Low/Normal IV → Buyers

Bull call spread: Buy a call, sell a higher call. You pay a fixed debit; max loss = that debit. Best when IV is low (options cheaper) and you want directional upside exposure with limited risk. For oversold + high-IV situations, outright long calls are expensive — spreads help reduce the cost.

Overbought + High IV → Bearish Sellers

Bear call spread: Sell a lower call, buy a higher call. Collect premium if the stock stays below your short strike. Max loss is defined. Best when a stock has run far and fast and IV is elevated — you're being paid to bet it doesn't go much higher.

Vol Spike Fade

Bear call spread on VXX/UVXY: Strike prices above the spike high. Collect premium if VIX declines. Never sell naked; always define the max loss with a higher bought call. Only use when VIX is genuinely stretched up (>30), as it is today.

Pre-Earnings Setups

Iron condor: Sell both a put spread and a call spread around the current price. Collects premium from elevated IV before earnings; profits if the stock stays in a range. Max loss = the wider spread width minus total premium collected. BRZE (Dec 8) and ACN (Dec 17) are candidates for this if IV is elevated into their reports.

RSI (14)
0–100 gauge; below 30 = oversold (stretched lower), above 70 = overbought (stretched higher). The number tells you how much momentum is one-sided, not which direction to trade.
Bollinger %B
0–1 scale; above 1 = price is above the upper band (overbought), below 0 = below the lower band (oversold). Used alongside RSI to confirm stretch.
Z-score
How many standard deviations (units of normal variation) price is from its 50-day average. ±2 or more = statistically unusual; ±3 = rare.
ATR
Average True Range — the typical daily price swing. Useful for sizing stops and comparing the size of a move to "normal" daily movement.
Reference Mean
The price level a stretched asset might revert toward — typically the 20-day, 50-day, or 200-day moving average. NOT a price target; just a statistical anchor.
Composite Stretch
A name that is extreme on RSI AND %B AND distance from moving averages AND consecutive losing days ranks higher than a name extreme on RSI alone. Three or more simultaneous signals = higher confidence.
IV (Implied Volatility)
The options market's estimate of how much a stock might move. High IV = expensive options (sellers get more premium); low IV = cheap options (buyers pay less).
Backwardation (VIX)
When near-term VIX futures are priced higher than further-out futures. This often signals a vol spike is happening and may fade — a classic setup for the VXX/UVXY fade trade.

9How to Read This Report / Guardrails

Read these before acting on anything in this report. They are not fine print — they are the most important section.

Reversion vs. Ruin — the most important filter:
  • A low RSI is necessary but not sufficient for a reversion setup. The key question is: "Is this a sentiment/positioning flush on a sound business, or is the business actually broken?"
  • Reject these: fraud, going-concern doubt, failed drug trials, dividend cuts on strained balance sheets, buyout repricing (stock pinned to deal price), or drops of 50%+ with no stabilization.
  • Keep these: names where the fundamentals are intact but price moved far on crowded sentiment, news-driven fear, or a one-time event — especially near prior support/resistance.
  • NVDA, AMD, SMH, QQQ, GLD: passed the filter — businesses sound, moves sentiment-driven. Bitcoin/IBIT: borderline — RSI extreme but institutional outflows are structural. Flagged with extra caution.
Proxy, K-1, and leverage flags (read before trading ETFs):
  • Proxy ETFs (GDX, GDXJ, SMH, XLE, XOP, GDX): These track companies (miners, chip makers, oil producers), not the commodity itself. They carry equity beta and can diverge from the underlying commodity. Confirm you understand what you're trading.
  • K-1 tax forms (USO, UNG, UGA, DBC, UCO, BOIL): These issue a Schedule K-1 at tax time, which complicates filings. Where possible, prefer no-K-1 alternatives: PDBC (broad commodity), COMB/BCI, BNO (Brent oil).
  • Leveraged ETFs (TQQQ, SQQQ, SOXL, UCO, BOIL, TBT, TMF/TMV): Volatility decay and roll effects mean these deviate from their long-run mean. Use for short-term tactical reversion only — multi-week holds should use the unlevered equivalent or equity-sector proxy.
  • Long-vol ETPs (VXX, UVXY, VIXY): NEVER treat low RSI as a "buy" signal. Structural roll decay grinds them lower over time. The only clean trade is fading the spike when RSI/price is stretched UP, as explained in Section 7.

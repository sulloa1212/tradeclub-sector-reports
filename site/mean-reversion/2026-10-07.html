This section maps market direction + implied volatility to the educational defined-risk structures mentioned in the tables. It also explains what each measure means.

Direction × IV → Educational Structure

Oversold + IV High Best for premium-selling structures that benefit if the stock stays above a strike:
• Cash-secured put — sell a put, collect premium, obligated to buy at strike
• Put credit spread — sell one put, buy a lower put; defines max loss
• Bull call spread — buy a call, sell a higher call; caps cost and profit
Oversold + IV Low Better for premium-buying structures (pay less for options when IV is low):
• Long call — buy a call outright; limited loss (premium paid)
• Bull call spread — reduces cost vs. outright long call
Overbought + IV High Best for premium-selling into the elevated IV that an extended run creates:
• Call credit spread — sell a call, buy a higher call; defines max loss
• Bear put spread — buy a put, sell a lower put; defines cost
Overbought + IV Low Premium-buying approach:
• Long put — buy a put outright; limited loss
• Bear put spread — reduces cost vs. outright long put

What Each Indicator Means

RSI (0–100 overbought/oversold gauge) Below 30 = oversold (heavy recent selling). Above 70 = overbought (heavy buying). Extreme readings (<20 / >80) suggest even more stretched positioning. RSI alone is not a signal — it needs confirmation from other measures.
Bollinger %B (where price sits in its band) Below 0 = price is below the lower band (deeply stretched down). Above 1 = price above the upper band (stretched up). A reading outside the bands on 2+ consecutive days is a stronger signal.
% vs 50-Day / 200-Day SMA (how far from its average price) A large negative % (e.g., −15%) means the asset is far below its 50-day average price — stretched to the downside. A large positive % means it's running well above average — stretched upward.
Z-Score (how many standard deviations from average) A z-score of ±2 or more means the price is unusually far from its recent average — a level that statistically tends to revert. Think of it as a "how unusual is this move" gauge.
IV (Implied Volatility — options market's uncertainty price) High IV = the options market expects large moves → option premiums are expensive → selling premium is more attractive. Low IV = options are cheap → buying premium is more attractive.
Reference Mean (the target for reversion) The price level this asset would "snap back" to if the setup works. Usually the 50-day or 200-day SMA. The mean is NOT a guaranteed target — it's an educational reference.
Golden rule: Always use defined-risk structures first — spreads and cash-secured puts, never naked options. Options can expire worthless (losing 100% of premium paid) or, on the short side without hedges, generate unlimited losses. Paper-trade any structure before using real capital.

🔪 Reversion vs. Ruin Filter A low RSI is necessary but not sufficient. Before considering a name, ask: Is the move driven by a structural break — fraud, bankruptcy, going-concern, failed drug trial, debt blowup? If yes → reject. Is the drop −50%+ in days with no stabilization? → reject. Is it pinned to a buyout/deal price? → reject. Keep only names where the move looks like sentiment excess over a sound business. Every name in this report passed that filter — but verify the thesis hasn't changed since this report was generated.
📉 Long-Vol ETPs — Upside Fade Only VXX, UVXY, VIXY: only trade these when stretched UP (a vol spike). Never treat a low RSI on these funds as an "oversold buy" — they are structurally decaying due to VIX futures contango. Futures-based funds often lose value even if market fear stays flat — they must constantly sell cheap expiring contracts to buy expensive future ones. A low VXX price is drift, not a setup.
⚡ Leveraged ETF Rules TQQQ, SQQQ, UCO, SCO, BOIL, KOLD, TBT, and any 2×/3× fund: use for short-term tactical reversion only. Leveraged products face daily rebalancing math that can cause significant losses in volatile, non-trending markets over several days. For multi-week holds, use the unlevered fund or equity-sector proxy instead.
🔄 Proxy vs. Spot GDX/GDXJ (gold miners), XLE/XOP (energy companies), URA (uranium companies), COPX (copper miners), MOO (agribusiness) — these track companies, not the commodity. They carry stock-market beta and can diverge significantly from the underlying commodity price. A low oil price doesn't guarantee XLE falls if earnings stay strong, and vice versa.
🧾 K-1 Tax Forms USO, UNG, UGA, DBC, UCO, BOIL, CPER, BNO, and other commodity partnerships issue a Schedule K-1 at tax time — which is more complex than a standard 1099. If tax simplicity matters, prefer alternatives: PDBC or BCI instead of DBC; XLE/XOP instead of USO; BOIL/KOLD only for very short-term holds.

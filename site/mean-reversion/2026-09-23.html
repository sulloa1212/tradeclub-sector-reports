7Options Structures Legend

This section explains the method — how signals map to educational structures and what the indicators measure. The results are in the tables above; this is the "how to read" key.

What the indicators measure:

RSI (0–100): Relative Strength Index — a speed-of-price gauge. Below 30 = oversold (selling may be exhausted). Above 70 = overbought (buying may be exhausted). Extreme: <20 or >80.
Bollinger %B (0–1): Where the price sits within its Bollinger Bands (a volatility envelope). Above 1 = price broke above upper band. Below 0 = price broke below lower band. Either is statistically unusual.
% from 50d / 200d SMA: How far price is from its 50-day or 200-day simple moving average (a smoothed "normal" price). A name stretched >15% below its 50d is historically unusual.
Z-score: How many standard deviations (statistical "jumps") the price is from its 50-day average. Z > 2 or < −2 is considered extreme.
IV (Implied Volatility): The options market's estimate of how much a stock will move. High IV = expensive options (favor selling premium); Low IV = cheap options (favor buying options).

8How to Read This Report / Guardrails

This section explains the filters applied in building the watchlist and the caveats every reader must understand before using any of this data.

The Reversion-vs-Ruin Test (applied to every name)
An oversold RSI is necessary — but not sufficient. Before any name enters the oversold table, it must pass these checks:
  • No structural break: Fraud, going-concern, failed trial, bankruptcy, accounting restatement — if any of these are present, the name is rejected as a "falling knife," not a reversion setup.
  • No buyout pin: If a company is in a deal, its price is pinned to deal terms, not its mean. Excluded.
  • No -50%+ in days without stabilization: Enormous single-move crashes (flash crashes, fraud revelations) are rejected unless price has clearly stabilized.
  • Sound business beneath the selloff: The move must look like sentiment or positioning excess over an otherwise intact business. RTX's $271B backlog, Bristol-Myers' dividend history, UDR's buy-rated analyst consensus — these are the kinds of anchors that make a low RSI worth watching.
Special handling: Volatility, Leveraged & Futures ETFs
  • Long-vol ETPs (VXX, UVXY, VIXY): Trade only when stretched UP on a vol spike — they revert down reliably as fear fades. A low RSI on VXX is not oversold — it is structural roll decay and contango. Never treat as a "buy the dip" setup.
  • Inverse-vol (SVXY, SVIX): They grind up in calm markets; their setup is recovery after a vol spike (the mirror of above). Catastrophic spike risk — handle with extreme care, small size.
  • Leveraged ETFs (TQQQ, UCO, SCO, BOIL, TBT, etc.): Volatility decay and daily resets mean these have no stable long-run mean. Use for short-term tactical reversion only (days, not weeks). Prefer the unlevered fund for multi-week holds.
  • K-1 tax flag: USO, UNG, UGA, DBC, UCO, BOIL, CPER, and many CurrencyShares ETFs issue a Schedule K-1, which complicates tax filing. Prefer "No K-1" alternatives where available: PDBC instead of DBC, BCI/COMB instead of broad commodity baskets.
  • Proxy vs. spot: GDX/GDXJ track gold-mining companies, not gold. URA tracks uranium-miner equities. XOP tracks E&P stocks. They carry equity beta and can diverge sharply from the underlying commodity. This is noted in every table where relevant.

7Options Structures Legend

This section explains the generic method — how direction and IV together point to a structure. The specific setups are in the tables above. All structures here are educational; paper-trade before using real money.

Oversold + High IV
Best: Cash-secured put (sell a put below current price; collect premium; own shares if assigned) or bull put spread (sell a put, buy a lower put — defined max loss). High IV inflates the premium you collect. Avoid buying long calls when IV is high — you overpay.
Oversold + Low/Mod IV
Best: Bull call spread (buy a lower-strike call, sell a higher-strike call — limits cost). Buying calls is cheaper when IV is low. The spread caps your upside but also caps your cost.
Overbought + High IV
Best: Bear call spread (sell a higher-strike call, buy an even-higher-strike call — collect premium, define max loss). High IV gives you a fatter credit. Avoid buying puts when IV is high — you overpay for protection.
Overbought + Low/Mod IV
Best: Bear put spread (buy a put, sell a lower put — limits cost). Cheaper when IV is low. Caps your downside profit but keeps cost manageable.
Range-bound + High IV
Best: Iron condor (sell a call spread above the range, sell a put spread below — collect premium from both sides). Profits if price stays inside the range through expiration.
RSI (14-day)
A 0–100 momentum gauge. Below 30 = oversold (selling may be exhausting). Above 70 = overbought (buying may be exhausting). Below 20 or above 80 = extreme. RSI alone is NOT a signal — always combine with other indicators.
Bollinger %B (20,2)
Shows price position within its Bollinger Band (a volatility envelope). Below 0 = price is below the lower band (stretched down). Above 1 = above the upper band (stretched up). More extreme than RSI alone; a strong second confirmation.
% vs 50/200-day SMA
How far price has moved from its moving average (a smoothed "mean"). A large negative % means the stock is well below its average and may be stretched. This is the "mean" the reversion thesis targets.
Z-score vs 50d
Standard deviations (units of "how unusual") from the 50-day average price. A Z-score of −2 means price is 2 standard deviations below average — statistically uncommon and often associated with reversion.
Implied Volatility (IV)
The market's forward-looking estimate of how much a stock will move, extracted from option prices. High IV = expensive options = premium-selling strategies shine. Low IV = cheap options = buying strategies make more sense.
Defined-Risk First
All structures above cap your maximum possible loss. Never sell naked (uncovered) options — especially when new. Always know the worst-case dollar loss before entering any options trade.

8How to Read This Report / Guardrails

The most important filter in mean-reversion trading: separating a stretched-but-recoverable name from a falling knife. Here's how we draw that line — and why it matters.

Reversion-vs-Ruin Filter
  • Reject (falling knife / ruin risk): Fraud, going-concern doubt, bankruptcy filing, failed Phase 3 drug trial, dividend elimination on a pure-income stock, major accounting restatement, or a drop of 50%+ in days with no stabilization. Low RSI on a broken business is not a setup — it's a sign the market is right.
  • Keep (sentiment/positioning excess): The move looks like crowded one-directional positioning, fear, or macro rotation — over an otherwise sound business or liquid ETF — ideally near a clear prior support/resistance level. The "mean" is well-defined and the catalyst has a plausible end (ceasefire, data print, Fed decision).
  • Event-driven extremes: Often the highest-quality reversion setups because the pre-shock price is a clear reference mean and the catalyst (oil spike, rate-hike fear) has a known resolution date (FOMC Sep 15–16).
Special-handling rules — always apply these
  • Long-vol ETPs (VXX, UVXY): Trade ONLY when stretched UP (RSI spike). They revert down as fear fades. Never buy them because RSI is "low" — structural roll decay grinds them lower forever. Low RSI on VXX is drift, not a setup.
  • Leveraged ETFs (UCO, BOIL, TQQQ, etc.): Volatility decay means they diverge from any stable mean over time. Use only for very short-term (days) tactical reversion. Prefer the unlevered fund or equity proxy for multi-week holds.
  • Futures-based ETFs (USO, UNG, UGA, etc.): Contango/backwardation roll costs make them unsuitable for long holds. The "price" you see is not the commodity price you think it is over time. Prefer DBO (oil) or PDBC (broad commodities) for longer holds — no K-1 and less roll drag.
  • K-1 tax flag: USO, UNG, UGA, DBC, UCO, BOIL, and similar commodity partnerships issue a Schedule K-1 instead of a 1099. This complicates taxes — consult your tax advisor and consider K-1-free alternatives (PDBC, BCI, DBO).
  • Proxy funds (GDX, XOP, URA, COPX): These track companies in a sector, not the underlying commodity directly. They carry equity beta — meaning they can diverge meaningfully from the spot commodity, especially on earnings, guidance, or sector-specific news.

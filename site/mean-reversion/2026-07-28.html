7 Options Structures Legend

This section explains the generic method — what each indicator measures and which structure fits which scenario. The specific readings and results are in the tables above.

RSI (14-day)
A 0–100 momentum gauge. Below 30 = oversold (recent selling may be exhausted). Above 70 = overbought (recent buying may be exhausted). Below 20 / above 80 = extreme. RSI alone is not a trade signal — it's a screening tool.
Bollinger %B (20,2)
Measures where price sits within its own 2-standard-deviation band. Below 0 = price below the lower band (extreme sell). Above 1 = price above the upper band (extreme buy). Confirms RSI when both fire together.
% from 50-d / 200-d SMA
How far price has drifted from its moving average (the "mean"). The 50-d is the short-term mean; 200-d is the long-term trend. Large % deviations are the "stretch" in mean reversion. The target for reversion is usually back to the 50-d SMA.
Z-score (50-d)
Number of standard deviations price is above or below its 50-day mean. A z-score of ±2 is roughly the same as %B outside the band. A z-score of ±3 is extreme (about 1-in-370 statistically, though real markets have fatter tails).
IV (Implied Volatility)
The options market's forward-looking expectation of price movement. High IV = expensive options = favor selling premium (credit spreads). Low IV = cheap options = favor buying (debit spreads). Reversion + high IV is the ideal setup for defined-risk credit structures.
Composite Rank
Names extreme on 3+ measures (RSI + %B + % from SMA + consecutive down/up days) rank higher than names extreme on RSI alone. Quality of the reversion thesis (catalyst fading, prior support nearby) further upgrades or downgrades the rank.
Oversold + High IV → Sell Premium
Cash-Secured Put (CSP): Sell a put below current price; collect premium; obligated to buy shares if assigned (at your chosen strike). Max loss = strike minus premium. Good when you'd want to own the stock anyway.

Bull Put Credit Spread: Sell a higher put, buy a lower put. Defined max risk = difference in strikes minus premium. Preferred for beginners — losses are capped.
Oversold + Low IV → Buy Debit Spread
Bull Call Debit Spread: Buy a call at a lower strike, sell a call at a higher strike. Pay a net debit. Max loss = debit paid. Max gain = difference in strikes minus debit. Suitable when IV is low (options are cheap) and you expect an upside bounce.
Overbought + High IV → Sell Premium
Bear Call Credit Spread: Sell a lower call, buy a higher call. Collect a net credit. Max risk = difference in strikes minus premium. Use when you expect price to fall or stay below the short strike. High IV makes the premium collected more attractive.
Overbought + Low IV → Buy Debit Spread
Bear Put Debit Spread: Buy a put at a higher strike, sell a put at a lower strike. Pay a net debit. Max loss = debit paid. Max gain = difference in strikes minus debit. Less common in current high-IV environment — but useful when vol is cheap.
Vol Spike Fade → Defined-Risk
Bear Call Spread on VXX/UVXY: When vol ETPs are spiking (RSI > 70), a bear call spread limits risk if the spike extends further before fading. Never sell naked calls on vol ETPs — unlimited risk. Confirm VXX spike is real and not driven by a sustained new-regime event.
Universal Rule — Defined Risk First
Every structure above caps your maximum loss at the outset. That's the point. Mean-reversion setups fail often — a stock that looks oversold can stay oversold or go to zero. If your loss is defined, you survive to trade another day. Always paper-trade first. Always verify prices and strikes against your brokerage before entering.

8 How to Read This / Guardrails

Reversion vs. Ruin — the most important filter in this report. An oversold RSI is a necessary condition, not a sufficient one. Before treating any name as a reversion candidate, ask: Is this a sentiment flush or a structural break?

  • Keep: Names where the move looks like excess fear or positioning over an otherwise sound business (e.g., LBRT gap-down on good earnings, chip sector contagion from Asian selloff).
  • Reject: Fraud, bankruptcy risk, going-concern doubts, dividend cuts, failed drug trials, accounting restatements, buyout repricing. Low RSI means nothing when the business is broken.
  • Flag with caution: Names on a prolonged structural downtrend with no stabilization (e.g., INTC's multi-year competitive challenges require extra evidence before treating as a clean reversion).

Special handling — vol, leveraged & futures-based ETFs.

  • Long-vol ETPs (VXX, UVXY, VIXY): Never buy these as "oversold" plays. Structural VIX-futures contango + leverage decay grind them lower forever. The only setup is fading spikes (selling premium when RSI is spiking above 70).
  • Leveraged ETFs (TQQQ, UCO, SCO, BOIL, KOLD, etc.): Volatility decay and roll costs mean they deviate from any long-run mean. Use only for short-term tactical reversion — multi-week holds prefer unlevered funds.
  • Futures-based funds (USO, UNG, UGA): Contango/backwardation roll can eat into returns even when the underlying commodity moves your way. Prefer equity proxies (XLE, XOP) for multi-week holds. USO, UNG, UGA, UCO, DBC issue a Schedule K-1 — consult your tax advisor; prefer PDBC or COMB if K-1 is a problem.
  • Proxy ETFs (GDX, URA, XOP, COPX, SIL): These track companies that produce commodities, not the commodities themselves. They carry equity beta — they can diverge significantly from spot prices during earnings season, currency moves, or sector rotations.

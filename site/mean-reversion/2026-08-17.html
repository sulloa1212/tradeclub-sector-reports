7Options Structures Legend

This legend explains the educational strategies mentioned in each table. Every structure here is defined-risk — your maximum loss is known in advance. Always paper-trade first.

RSI (0–100 momentum gauge) Below 30 = oversold (selling may be stretched); above 70 = overbought (buying may be stretched). Below 20 / above 80 = extreme. RSI alone is not a buy or sell signal — it's a screening tool.
Bollinger %B (band position gauge) Measures where price sits within its Bollinger Band (a moving-average envelope). %B < 0 = price below lower band (stretched low); %B > 1 = price above upper band (stretched high). Combined with RSI, it adds a second stretch confirmation.
Z-score (standard deviations from mean) How many standard deviations (units of typical daily variation) the price is above or below its 50-day average. Z > 2 or < −2 = statistically unusual; Z > 3 = rare extreme. A larger number = more stretched.
ATR (average true range) The average daily price swing for a stock. If a stock drops 5× its ATR in a few days, that's statistically abnormal. Used here to confirm that a move is an extreme, not just normal noise.
Bull Call Spread (oversold, any IV) Buy a lower-strike call, sell a higher-strike call. Max loss = net premium paid. Best when you expect a moderate bounce, not a huge move. The sold call caps your upside but reduces cost.
Bear Put Spread (overbought, any IV) Buy a higher-strike put, sell a lower-strike put. Max loss = net premium paid. Best when you expect a moderate pullback. Defined-risk alternative to shorting the stock.
Call Credit Spread (overbought + high IV) Sell a lower-strike call, buy a higher-strike call. You collect premium upfront. Max loss = spread width minus premium. Best when IV is elevated (premium is expensive to sell) and you expect the stock to pull back or stall. IV crush benefits the seller.
Put Credit Spread / Cash-Secured Put (oversold + high IV) Sell a put (at or below current price) and collect premium. A cash-secured put means you hold enough cash to buy the stock if assigned. A put credit spread adds a long put below for defined risk. Best when IV is elevated and you're comfortable owning the stock at the strike price.
IV (implied volatility) — high vs low IV measures how much the options market expects the stock to move. High IV = expensive options. When IV is high, selling premium (credit spreads) is generally more efficient — you collect more. When IV is low, buying premium (debit spreads) is cheaper. The reversion setup is most powerful when stretched RSI + high IV align: the stock wants to revert AND premium is rich to sell.
IV Crush (post-earnings) After an earnings release, IV almost always collapses — even if the stock moves a lot. If you buy options into earnings (paying high IV) and the stock moves your way, you may still lose money because the premium deflated. This is why the tables flag earnings dates and suggest waiting for post-earnings entry or selling premium before the event (with defined risk).

8How to Read This / Guardrails

Before acting on any setup in this report, run through this checklist mentally. These are the filters that separate a reversion opportunity from a trap.

Reversion vs. Ruin Filter (most important):
A low RSI is necessary — but NOT sufficient. Before treating an oversold reading as a setup, ask: Is this a structural break? Check for fraud, going-concern warnings, dividend cuts, failed drug trials, bankruptcies, or accounting issues. Is the stock down 50%+ with no stabilization? That's a falling knife, not a reversion. Is this a buyout repricing? The price is pinned to a deal, not its mean. If any of these apply, the setup is disqualified regardless of how low the RSI is.
Long-Vol ETPs (VXX, UVXY, VIXY, UVIX) — upside spikes only:
Never treat a low VXX/UVXY price as an "oversold" opportunity to buy. These ETPs suffer from structural roll decay (VIX futures contango) that grinds them lower over time. A low RSI on VXX is simply drift, not a setup. The only mean-reversion play in these instruments is fading them when they spike UP on a volatility event — and today, with VIX at ~14, no such spike exists.
Leveraged and Futures-Based ETFs (UCO, SCO, BOIL, KOLD, TBT, TQQQ, 3x anything):
Volatility decay + futures roll effects cause these to drift from any long-run mean. Use only for short-term (days, not weeks) tactical ideas. For multi-week holds, use the unlevered equivalent (e.g., XLE instead of ERX, GLD instead of UGLD).
Proxy vs. Spot flag:
GDX/GDXJ track gold-mining companies — they carry equity beta and can diverge significantly from spot gold prices. XOP and OIH track oil-company stocks — same principle. URA/URNM track uranium miners. COPX tracks copper miners. None of these are direct commodity exposure — they move with the commodity and also with their own company-level risks.
K-1 Tax Flag:
USO, UNG, UGA, BNO, DBC, UCO, BOIL, and some CurrencyShares ETFs issue a Schedule K-1 at tax time — more complex than a standard 1099. If you prefer to avoid K-1s, use no-K-1 alternatives: PDBC or BCI for broad commodities; DBO for oil (no K-1); no clean no-K-1 single-commodity nat-gas alternative with liquid options.

7Options Structures Legend

This section explains the indicators and structures used in the tables above — it is the method, not the conclusions. Results for each specific name are in the tables above.

Indicator definitions (what each number means):
RSI (14)
Relative Strength Index — a 0–100 momentum gauge. Below 30 = recent selling extreme (oversold); above 70 = recent buying extreme (overbought). Below 20 / above 80 = more severe extreme. RSI alone is not sufficient — requires 2+ confirmations.
Bollinger %B (20,2)
Shows where price sits within a 2-standard-deviation band around a 20-day average. Below 0 = price is below the lower band (stretched down); above 1 = price is above the upper band (stretched up).
Z-score / % from SMA
How many % (or standard deviations) the current price is from its 50-day or 200-day simple moving average. The further below the SMA, the more stretched the downside move; the further above, the more stretched the upside move.
Stochastic %K
A 0–100 oscillator comparing closing price to recent highs/lows. Below 20 = oversold; above 80 = overbought. Used as a secondary confirmation alongside RSI.
CCI (Commodity Channel Index)
Measures how far price is from its statistical average. Below −100 = oversold; above +100 = overbought. A third confirmation when present.
MACD (12,26,9)
Moving average convergence/divergence — a trend and momentum indicator. MACD below zero = bearish trend; above zero = bullish. Used to confirm whether the trend is consistent with the RSI signal.
Direction + IV → Defined-Risk Structure mapping (educational only):
Oversold + High IV
Cash-secured put (sell a put at a price you'd be willing to own the stock); OR put credit spread (sell a put, buy a lower put — limits max risk). High IV means you collect more premium for selling. Always defined-risk first.
Oversold + Low IV
Bull call spread (buy a call, sell a higher call) — buying premium when it's cheap. The long call benefits from the upside move; the short call caps profit but reduces cost.
Overbought + High IV
Call credit spread (sell a call, buy a higher call) or bear put spread (buy a put, sell a lower put). High IV makes sold calls more valuable — again, premium selling is favored.
Overbought + Low IV
Bear put spread — buy a put near ATM, sell a lower put. Defined cost, limited profit. Use when premium is cheap and direction is clear.
Defined-risk first rule
Spreads (buying AND selling an option at the same time) cap your maximum loss to the net debit or net premium collected minus the spread width. Always use defined-risk structures; never sell naked options without full understanding of the unlimited-loss risk.
IV (Implied Volatility)
The market's forecast of how much the stock might move. High IV = expensive options (premium selling has an edge). Low IV = cheap options (buying premium is more cost-effective). Check your brokerage's options chain for the IV percentile (IVP) — above 50 is elevated, above 80 is very high.

8Guardrails & How to Read This Report

The reversion-vs-ruin filter — the most important section in this report:
  • Keep names where the selloff looks like sentiment/positioning excess over a structurally sound business — a broad market rout, an earnings miss on one quarter, a macro shock that is now fading.
  • Reject names with structural breaks: fraud, bankruptcy risk, going-concern doubt, failed drug trials with no pipeline, permanent competitive displacement. A low RSI does NOT fix a broken business.
  • Caution flag for INTC: shareholder lawsuit + structural competitive challenges mean this is higher on the ruin-risk scale than the other semi names. Use smallest possible size, defined-risk only.
  • Caution flag for MRVL: the Lumentum deal is a structural change — analyst de-ratings reflect dilution concerns, not just sentiment. Partial reversion is the realistic thesis, not a full return to prior highs.
  • Never average down into a falling knife. Wait for a stabilization candle (a session that closes flat or up after the decline stalls).
Proxy, K-1, and leverage flags (know these before trading):
  • K-1 tax forms — USO, UNG, UGA, UCO, BOIL, most commodity ETFs issue a Schedule K-1, which complicates tax filing. Prefer no-K-1 alternatives where available (PDBC for broad commodities; XLE/XOP for oil equity exposure).
  • Proxy ≠ spot — GDX/GDXJ tracks gold-mining companies (equity beta included), not gold itself. URA tracks uranium companies. XOP/XLE tracks energy stocks. These can diverge sharply from the underlying commodity in a risk-off environment.
  • Leveraged & futures-based ETFs (UCO, BOIL, TQQQ, TBT, etc.) suffer from daily leverage reset decay. Use for short-term tactical plays only; never hold multi-week through volatile regimes.
  • Long-vol ETFs (VXX, UVXY) — special rule: As explained in Section 6, only fade these when they spike UP. Never treat a low RSI on VXX or UVXY as an oversold buy — it is structural decay.
Verify before trading — mandatory checklist:
  1. Confirm the current price against your live brokerage quote — do not use the figures in this report directly.
  2. Confirm RSI on your own charting platform (TradingView, ThinkorSwim, etc.) — screener RSI may differ by a day or methodology.
  3. Check upcoming earnings dates in your brokerage's earnings calendar — dates in this report are either confirmed from search or marked "unconfirmed — verify."
  4. Check options open interest and bid-ask spreads for the specific strike you intend to use — a wide spread can erase any theoretical edge.
  5. Paper-trade any new structure at least 3–5 times before using real capital.
  6. Never risk more than you can afford to lose on a single trade — mean reversion setups fail ~30–40% of the time even when they look clean.
